Tokens at Scale-I : From One Prompt to Thousands of Users

2026-09-28

One prompt is a demo. A thousand prompts a second is an engineering problem.


Cover image: DeepInfra

In my last post, Squeezing the SM, I talked about making a single kernel fast: the roofline, memory coalescing, tensor cores, and how a kernel spends most of its life waiting on memory. That naturally leads here, because the biggest job those kernels get today is one where waiting on memory is the whole story. This series is about what happens when those kernels are put to work doing the thing most GPUs in the world are now bought for: generating text from a large language model.

I want to start with the picture everyone has in their head. A user types a prompt, the model thinks for a moment, and a reply streams back. One person, one request, one answer. If you’ve ever run a model on your own machine, that’s exactly what it looks like, and it works fine.

Now put that model behind a product. There isn’t one user anymore, there are hundreds or thousands, all at the same time. Their requests arrive whenever they like. One person pastes a 30-page contract, another types “hi”. One answer is ten tokens, another is two thousand. Every one of them wants the first word in well under a second and the rest streaming faster than they can read. And underneath all of it sit GPUs that you’re paying for by the hour, whether they’re busy or not. An H100 that spends 99% of its time waiting costs exactly as much as one that’s flat out.

That last sentence is the whole job of an inference engineer, really. You pay for GPU-hours, so the game is to get as many useful tokens out of every hour as possible, without making any user wait longer than they’re willing to. Do it badly and a service with a hundred requests a second needs something like 150 GPUs. Do it well and the same traffic fits on about seven. By the end of this post you’ll be able to do that arithmetic yourself.

When I started reading about this, I expected the hard part to be the model. It isn’t. The hard part is everything around it. So in this first part I’ll walk the whole serving stack, one layer at a time, with vLLM, the most widely used open-source serving engine, as my guide. We’ll start with what one request actually does to a GPU (prefill, decode and the KV cache, with real numbers), then look at how a server packs thousands of requests together (every popular batching technique), how it fits their memory (PagedAttention), how it spreads a model too big for one GPU (sharding), how a fleet of servers splits the traffic (load balancing), and finally how you tune all of it for maximum utilisation. The later parts of the series each zoom into one of these, and here’s the route through this one:

Part 1: From One User to Thousands

One User, One Request

Let’s follow the simple case first, because every cost in production comes from something that happens here.

Your prompt arrives as text, and the first thing that happens is that a tokenizer chops it into tokens: sub-word pieces from a fixed vocabulary, roughly three quarters of a word each in English. “The capital of France is” becomes five tokens, each mapped to an integer ID. From then on, the model never sees text. It sees a sequence of IDs, turns each into a vector (an embedding), and pushes the whole sequence through a deep stack of identical transformer blocks.

Almost every model you’d chat with today is a decoder-only transformer, the GPT shape, and it’s worth looking at what’s inside it, because each piece turns into a cost later:


Thirty-two identical blocks, each an attention layer and an MLP. The MLPs hold over 80% of the weights; attention is where the KV cache comes from. The 70B model stacks 80 of these blocks.

Inside each block, two things happen. Attention lets every token look at the tokens before it and pull in what’s relevant. Then a feed-forward network (the MLP), two or three big matrix multiplies, transforms each token’s vector on its own. The final layer turns the last token’s vector into a score for every token in the vocabulary, and sampling picks one: the highest score, or a random draw shaped by a temperature and a top-k or top-p cut. That chosen token is the model’s answer to one question only: what comes next?

To get a second token, the model has to ask again, with the first token now part of the input. And again for the third. This is autoregressive generation, and it’s the defining constraint of the whole field:


Every new token depends on all the tokens before it, so the steps can’t run in parallel. A 500-token answer is 500 trips through the model.

That loop is why your answer streams in word by word instead of arriving all at once. It’s not a UI choice. The model genuinely doesn’t know the tenth word until it has produced the ninth.

Now Make It Thousands

The simple picture has a property that’s easy to miss: between your requests, the GPU does nothing. It answers you, then sits idle while you read, think and type. For a model on your own laptop that’s fine, because the laptop is yours anyway.

A production server never gets that luxury, and never wants it. Here’s what two seconds of traffic on a single server can look like:


In production, requests overlap constantly, and no two are the same shape. The server’s job is to run all of them on the same GPUs at once.

Three things change when you go from one user to thousands, and they’re the three themes of this post.

The first is concurrency. At any moment there are dozens or hundreds of requests in flight, each at a different point in its life: some just arrived, some halfway through a long answer, some about to finish. The server has to run them together, on the same GPUs, without any of them noticing the others.

The second is variability. Prompts range from a few tokens to a hundred thousand, and nobody knows how long an answer will be until the model decides to stop. A scheduler that treats requests as interchangeable will get this badly wrong.

The third is cost, and it’s the one that drives everything else. Cloud GPUs are rented by the hour. Rental prices move around a lot, but call it $3 an hour for an H100 to keep the arithmetic easy. That price doesn’t change when the GPU is idle, and it doesn’t change when it’s busy. So the only number you control is how many tokens you get out of each hour. Every technique in the rest of this post is, in the end, a way of raising that number while keeping each user’s latency inside a target.

Enter vLLM

This is the problem a serving engine solves, and the one I’ll lean on throughout is vLLM, mostly because it’s the one whose design docs and papers taught me the most. It started in 2023 at UC Berkeley’s Sky Computing Lab, alongside a paper by Woosuk Kwon and colleagues that introduced PagedAttention (Part 4 of this post is about it). It’s open source under the Apache 2.0 license, it’s now a PyTorch Foundation project with hundreds of contributors, and it runs on NVIDIA and AMD GPUs, TPUs and more. If you use a hosted open-weights model today, there’s a good chance vLLM, or something built on its ideas, is generating your tokens.

From the outside, vLLM is almost boring to use, which is the point. One command downloads a model, loads it onto the GPU and starts an HTTP server that speaks the same API as OpenAI’s:

The interesting part is the second flag. gpu-memory-utilization 0.90 tells vLLM it may claim 90% of the GPU’s memory. It loads the weights, measures how much room the model needs for its working buffers, and hands everything else to the KV cache, which you’ll meet in Part 2. That one design choice says a lot about what vLLM thinks matters.

Inside, it’s a small distributed system even on a single machine. Since early 2025 vLLM runs on its rebuilt V1 engine, and the project’s own docs draw it like this for a model split over four GPUs:


Three kinds of process: an API server that speaks HTTP and handles text, an engine core that makes every scheduling decision, and one worker per GPU that just runs the model. Diagram: vLLM project, Apache 2.0

The API server deals with everything that isn’t GPU work: parsing requests, tokenising prompts, turning output tokens back into text and streaming them to users. It’s a separate process so that all this Python busywork never stalls the GPU. The engine core is the brain. It holds the scheduler and the KV cache manager, and it runs a tight loop. The GPU workers, one per GPU, run whatever forward pass the engine core tells them to.

The loop is the thing to understand, because everything else in this post happens inside it:


Every few milliseconds, the same five steps. Batching, PagedAttention and preemption are all decisions made somewhere in this loop.

Each turn of the loop is one step: one forward pass of the model over a batch of tokens drawn from many different requests. The scheduler decides which requests get to run and how many of their tokens to process. The KV cache manager finds memory for them. The workers run the model. Every request that’s generating gets exactly one new token, which streams back to its user. Then the loop goes round again, a hundred or so times a second.

To see why this loop is designed the way it is, we need to look at what a forward pass actually costs. That’s where the two halves of every request come in.

Part 2: Prefill, Decode and the KV Cache

Where the KV Cache Comes From

Look again at the autoregressive loop and something should bother you. At step 500, the input is 499 tokens of prompt and answer plus one new token. If every step pushed the whole sequence through the whole model again, generation would get slower and slower, with the total work growing with the square of the length. And it would be almost entirely wasted, because nothing about the first 499 tokens has changed.

To see what can be saved, you need to know what attention actually computes. Each token’s vector is projected into three new vectors: a query (what this token is looking for), a key (what this token offers to others) and a value (what this token hands over when it’s chosen). A token’s output is a weighted mix of the values of all earlier tokens, weighted by how well its query matches their keys. In a decoder, the matching is causal: a token may only look backwards.


Causal self-attention for two positions. Each token’s query is matched against the keys of itself and earlier tokens, never later ones, and the match weights mix the corresponding values. Diagram: dvgodoy via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 4.0

Here’s the key observation. Because attention is causal, a token’s key and value depend only on that token and the tokens before it. Once computed, they never change. Token 37’s key at step 38 is exactly the same as its key at step 500. So instead of recomputing them, the model computes each token’s keys and values once, in every layer, and stores them. That store is the KV cache.


The cache turns repeated computation into a memory read. That trade is the heart of LLM inference.

With the cache, each generation step only has to push one new token through the model. Its query attends over all the cached keys and values, its own key and value get appended to the cache, and the next step does the same. The quadratic recomputation disappears. What replaces it is the theme of this entire series: memory. The cache grows by one entry per layer per token, for every request being served, and it has to sit in GPU memory for as long as the request lives.

Two Workloads in One Request

The KV cache splits every request into two phases with completely different personalities.

The first is prefill. When your prompt arrives, none of its keys and values exist yet, so the model processes all the prompt tokens at once, in a single forward pass, filling the cache for every layer and producing the first output token at the end. All the tokens are known up front, so this is beautifully parallel: every weight matrix multiplies a whole matrix of token vectors at a time.

The second is decode. From the first output token onwards, the model runs one step per new token. Each step pushes a single token through the network, reading the whole cache in every attention layer and appending one new entry. Every weight matrix now multiplies a single vector.


Prefill is one big burst. Decode is a long trickle of tiny steps. The first token appears exactly where one ends and the other begins.

Same model, same weights, same request, and yet prefill is a matrix-times-matrix workload while decode is matrix-times-vector. If you read the CUDA post, you can probably already guess which one the GPU likes.

Prefill and Decode on the Roofline

Remember arithmetic intensity: FLOPs performed per byte moved from memory. For one of the model’s weight matrices, with dimensions d × d in 16-bit precision, processing T tokens costs about 2·T·d² FLOPs (a multiply and an add per weight per token), and the weights themselves are 2·d² bytes, read once. So the intensity is roughly T FLOPs per byte: it’s simply the number of tokens pushed through at once.

Put that on an H100, whose BF16 tensor cores peak at about 989 TFLOP/s and whose HBM3 delivers 3.35 TB/s. The ridge point sits at 989 ÷ 3.35 ≈ 295 FLOPs per byte.


Prefill of any real prompt sits past the ridge, limited by compute. Decode sits far down the bandwidth slope, limited by how fast the weights can be read.

Prefill of a 2,000-token prompt has an intensity around 2,000, well past the ridge, so it’s compute-bound. Decode for a single user has an intensity of about one: each weight is fetched from HBM, used for one multiply-add with the one token’s vector, and thrown away. A side effect worth noticing: a prompt shorter than about 300 tokens doesn’t reach the ridge either, so short prefills are memory-bound too.

There’s one more piece, and it’s the one that makes inference hard: attention during decode. Every request has its own KV cache, and the new token’s query has to read all of it. Those bytes belong to that request alone, so nothing can share them, and attention’s intensity stays down near one no matter how many requests you serve at once. Hold that thought, because it’s about to show up in the numbers.

Prefill and Decode in Numbers

The roofline tells you which wall you’re against. To feel how hard, turn it into milliseconds. Take Llama 3 8B in BF16 on one H100. The model has about 8.03 billion parameters, so its weights are 16.06 GB, and every forward pass has to read all of them at least once. For each pass there are two times worth computing: how long the maths would take if memory were free (FLOPs ÷ 989 TFLOP/s), and how long the memory traffic would take if maths were free (bytes ÷ 3.35 TB/s). Whichever is longer is the one you’re stuck with.

Here’s prefill of a 2,048-token prompt. Each token costs about 2 × 8.03 billion ≈ 16 GFLOPs for the weight matrices, so 2,048 tokens cost about 32.9 TFLOPs, plus about 1.1 TFLOPs for attention among the prompt tokens: 34 TFLOPs, or 34.4 ms at peak. The memory side is the 16.06 GB of weights plus about 0.27 GB of fresh KV cache written out, 4.9 ms. Compute takes seven times longer than memory. Prefill is compute-bound, and the tensor cores are genuinely busy. (Real kernels reach maybe 60 to 70% of peak, so call it 50 ms in practice.)

Now decode for one user, with 2,048 tokens of context already in the cache. One token costs about 16 GFLOPs for the weights plus about 1 GFLOP of attention, 17 GFLOPs, which takes 0.017 ms at peak. But that step still reads all 16.06 GB of weights, plus the request’s 0.27 GB of KV cache: 4.9 ms. Memory takes 280 times longer than the maths. For 99.6% of that step, the most expensive arithmetic units ever built are waiting for data.


Prefill keeps the tensor cores busy. Decode leaves them idle at every batch size, because every sequence brings its own KV cache to read.

The bottom two rows are the interesting ones. Batch 64 users into one decode step and the weights are still read once, so the maths grows 64 times while the weight traffic doesn’t grow at all. That’s the effect batching is built on. But look at the memory bar: it doubles, from 4.9 to 9.9 ms, because 64 requests each bring 0.27 GB of KV cache that must be read on every step. Each extra 2K-token request adds about 0.08 ms of KV reads but only 0.017 ms of maths, so the memory bar always grows faster than the compute bar. Even with the GPU’s KV memory completely full, at 240 requests, decode is still six times memory-bound.

That’s worth sitting with. With realistic context lengths, decode never becomes compute-bound, however much you batch. The weights can be shared across a batch; the KV cache can’t. This one fact explains why so much of inference engineering is really memory engineering.

The Weight Wall: Why Batching Is Everything

Turn the decode row into a speed. At 4.9 ms per step, one user gets at most about 200 tokens per second, an upper bound set purely by memory bandwidth. That’s comfortably faster than anyone reads, but it’s a terrible use of the hardware.

Now batch. Each step reads all 16 GB of weights once, regardless of how many requests it serves. If eight users’ tokens go through the same step together, that one read now produces eight tokens:


Batch 64: about 31 times the total throughput, while each user’s own speed only halves. These are bandwidth-only estimates, but the shape is exactly what real systems see.

Going from one user to sixty-four multiplies the tokens produced by about 31, while every individual user still gets a hundred tokens a second, far faster than anyone reads. The GPU costs the same per hour either way. This is batching, and it’s the single most important idea in LLM serving. It’s also why a production server isn’t just a scaled-up version of your laptop: thousands of users aren’t a burden on the hardware, they’re what makes it efficient.

So the obvious move is to batch as hard as possible, and two things stop you. The first is that requests don’t arrive or finish together, which is Part 3. The second is memory, which brings us back to the cache.

How Big the KV Cache Gets

Each token stores a key and a value vector in every layer, for every key-value head. In 16-bit precision, that’s

KV bytes per token = 2 × layers × KV heads × head dimension × 2 bytes

The “KV heads” term is where model design comes in. Attention is split into heads, parallel attention computations that each look for different relationships. In the original transformer, every query head has its own key and value head: multi-head attention (MHA). Noam Shazeer pointed out in 2019 that decode is so memory-bound that you could share one key and value head across all the query heads, multi-query attention (MQA), at some cost in quality. The compromise that most current models use is grouped-query attention (GQA), from Ainslie and colleagues at Google:


Multi-head, grouped-query and multi-query attention. Fewer key and value heads means a smaller KV cache to store and read on every decode step. Figure: Ainslie et al. (2023), arXiv:2305.13245, CC BY 4.0

The difference this makes is dramatic. Here are three real models:


Almost the same model size, a quarter of the KV cache: Llama 3 8B’s grouped-query attention shares each key and value head across four query heads.

Llama 2 7B, with full multi-head attention, stores half a megabyte per token. Llama 3 8B, a slightly bigger model, stores a quarter of that, purely because of GQA. And these numbers get large fast: one 8K-token conversation with Llama 3 70B holds 2.5 GiB of cache, and a single 128K-token context holds 40 GiB, half an H100 for one user. More recent designs push even harder, such as DeepSeek’s multi-head latent attention (MLA), which compresses keys and values into a small shared latent vector, and KV caches stored in FP8. The motivation is always the same: the cache is the thing that runs out.

The vLLM team measured exactly this on a real deployment. Serving a 13-billion-parameter model on a 40 GB A100, the memory splits like this:


Weights are a fixed cost. Everything else is the KV cache, and it’s the part that decides how many requests can run at once. Figure: Kwon et al. (2023), arXiv:2309.06180, CC BY 4.0

How Many Requests Fit

Put the two halves together on an 80 GB H100 serving Llama 3 8B. The weights take about 15 GiB, a few GiB go to activations and workspace, and roughly 60 GiB is left for KV cache. How many requests fit depends entirely on how long they are:


The same GPU holds 240 short conversations or 3 very long ones. Context length eats the batch.

This is the central tension of LLM serving, and it’s worth stating plainly. Decode is only efficient when the batch is large, and the batch can only be as large as the KV cache allows. Every extra token of context in every conversation takes room that could have held another user. Hold on to this; it’s the reason Part 4 exists.

Measuring It: TTFT, TPOT, Throughput and Goodput

Before getting into the techniques, it helps to be precise about what “fast” means, because a serving system has to satisfy two audiences at once: each user, and whoever pays for the GPUs.


What each user feels. Throughput and goodput are the same timeline seen from the operator’s side.

Time to first token (TTFT) is how long a user waits before anything appears: queueing, plus prefill. It’s dominated by prompt length and by how busy the server is. Time per output token (TPOT), or its per-token cousin inter-token latency (ITL), is how fast the answer streams once it starts, and it’s set by decode: by how big the batch is and how much KV cache each step has to read. A TPOT of 50 ms is 20 tokens a second, faster than anyone reads; at 200 ms the stream visibly stutters. End-to-end latency is TTFT plus TPOT for every token after the first.

The operator cares about throughput: total tokens per second across all users, which is what decides the cost per token. And these pull in opposite directions. Bigger batches raise throughput and slow every individual stream. Fitting a new user’s prefill into a running batch gets them their first token sooner but stalls everyone else’s decode for that step. There’s no setting that wins on everything.

So modern systems are judged by goodput, a term the DistServe authors made popular: the rate of requests that actually meet their latency targets, say a TTFT under half a second and a TPOT under 50 ms for 90% of requests. Throughput that violates the targets doesn’t count. These targets are called service level objectives (SLOs), and goodput is the number every technique in the rest of this post is trying to raise.

Part 3: Every Batching Technique

We know batching is what makes decode efficient. The hard part is doing it when requests arrive at random and finish at random. There’s a progression of techniques here, and each one fixes the most painful problem left by the one before.

Static Batching

The simplest approach is the one you’d write first. Collect a fixed number of requests, say eight, pad their prompts to the same length, run prefill on all of them together, then run decode steps for the whole batch until every request is finished. Then start the next batch.

It works, and for offline jobs where all the prompts are known up front (scoring a dataset, generating summaries overnight) it’s fine. For live traffic it falls apart, for two reasons. Requests that arrive while a batch is running have to wait for the whole batch to finish, however long that takes. And within the batch, every request is held hostage by the longest one. Suppose the eight answers are 50, 120, 200, 300, 400, 600, 900 and 2,000 tokens long. The batch runs for 2,000 steps, but the average request only needed 571 of them, so about 71% of the slot-steps are wasted, producing padding for requests that have already finished. On top of that, padding prompts to a common length wastes prefill compute too.

Dynamic Batching

Dynamic batching fixes the arrival problem. Instead of waiting for exactly N requests, the server launches a batch when it has N requests or when a short timer runs out, whichever comes first, so a lone request at 3 a.m. doesn’t wait forever. This is what general-purpose inference servers like NVIDIA’s Triton Inference Server do for image classifiers and recommendation models, and for those it’s excellent, because every request costs the same: one forward pass.

For LLMs it only fixes half the problem. The batch is still formed once and runs until its longest member finishes. Both static and dynamic batching are request-level batching: the unit of scheduling is the whole request. And an LLM request isn’t one forward pass, it’s hundreds of them, of unknown number.

Continuous Batching

The fix came from a 2022 paper by Gyeong-In Yu and colleagues at Seoul National University, describing a system called Orca. Their idea was iteration-level scheduling: make the scheduling decision at every step instead of once per batch. After each forward pass, any request that just produced its end-of-sequence token leaves the batch and frees its slot, and any waiting request can take that slot on the very next step. The batch is never “done”; it’s a running pool that requests flow through. This is now universally called continuous batching (NVIDIA calls it in-flight batching).


Request-level batching waits for the slowest request. Continuous batching refills a slot the moment it frees up.

There was a technical obstacle, and Orca’s second idea, selective batching, solved it. Requests in a continuous batch are at different positions (one is on token 12, another on token 1,800), so their attention computations have different shapes and can’t be stacked into one neat tensor. But the linear layers, which hold most of the weights and most of the FLOPs, don’t care about position at all: each token’s vector is multiplied by the same matrices. So Orca flattens all the tokens from all the requests into one long list for the linear layers, and splits back out per request only for attention. That trick is still how every serving engine works today.

The payoff was enormous. The Orca paper reported 36.9 times the throughput of NVIDIA’s FasterTransformer at the same latency on a GPT-3-sized model, and a widely read 2023 benchmark from Anyscale found continuous batching in vLLM gave up to 23 times the throughput of naive static batching. Every serious engine does it now: vLLM, SGLang, Hugging Face’s TGI and NVIDIA’s TensorRT-LLM.

The Prefill Problem

Continuous batching raises a new question: when a new request joins, what happens to its prefill? Its prompt might be 4,000 tokens, and those all have to be processed before it can join the decode pool.

Early systems had two options, and both are bad. Prefill-first scheduling (what vLLM did by default in its early days) runs the new request’s prefill as soon as it arrives, and since a step is one forward pass, every decoding request sits in that step too. On Llama 3 8B, a 4,096-token prefill is about 71 TFLOPs of maths, so that step takes about 71 ms instead of the usual 10. Every one of the 64 users currently streaming an answer sees their next token arrive seven times late. If prompts arrive often enough, streams stutter visibly. Decode-first scheduling does the opposite: finish what’s running, admit new prefills only when there’s a gap. Streams stay smooth, but new users wait, and TTFT balloons.

Both are a choice between hurting TTFT and hurting TPOT. The way out is to stop treating a prompt as one indivisible unit.

Chunked Prefill

Chunked prefill, from the Sarathi and Sarathi-Serve papers by Amey Agrawal and colleagues at Microsoft Research India and Georgia Tech, splits a long prompt into chunks of, say, 1,024 tokens, and processes one chunk per step, mixed in with everyone else’s decode tokens. Each step has a token budget: a cap on how many tokens it may process in total. The decodes take one token each, and whatever’s left of the budget goes to prefill chunks.


Same total work, spread so that no single step is long enough to stall anyone. The decodes ride along in every chunked step.

Two good things happen at once. First, no step is ever huge, so inter-token latency stays smooth: about 19 ms per step here instead of a 71 ms spike. That’s what Sarathi-Serve calls stall-free batching. Second, and this is the subtle one, the decode tokens become almost free. Remember that decode is memory-bound: the expensive part is reading 16 GB of weights. A step that processes 1,024 prompt tokens already reads those weights and is compute-bound anyway, so adding 64 decode tokens to it barely changes its duration. The decodes piggyback on the prefill’s weight reads, and the GPU spends that step doing what it’s good at. Mixing the two kinds of work makes every step closer to the ridge point from both sides.

The tradeoff lives in the size of the token budget. A small budget means short, smooth steps (good TPOT) but long prompts take many steps to get through (worse TTFT) and each chunk re-reads the weights. A large budget finishes prompts faster but makes steps longer. In vLLM this knob is the maximum number of batched tokens per step, and tuning it is one of the first things you do for a given model and latency target.

The Scheduler: Who Runs, Who Waits, Who Gets Kicked Out

In vLLM’s V1 engine, chunked prefill isn’t a special mode any more; it falls out of how the scheduler thinks. The scheduler doesn’t distinguish prefill from decode at all. Every request simply has a number of tokens it knows about (prompt plus generated so far) and a number it has computed. Each step, the scheduler hands out the token budget: one token to each running request that’s decoding, and the remainder to requests that still have prompt tokens to catch up on, in first-come first-served order. A request with 3,000 uncomputed prompt tokens and a budget of 2,048 with 64 decodes running gets 1,984 tokens this step and the rest next step. The scheduler’s decision for a step is literally a map from request ID to number of tokens. It’s a lovely simplification.

The scheduler also decides admission. A waiting request is only admitted if the KV cache manager can find memory for its tokens, and there are caps on the number of running requests. And sometimes things go wrong after admission: requests are admitted with no idea how long their answers will be, so as everyone’s cache grows, memory can run out mid-flight. Then the scheduler must preempt someone. vLLM picks the lowest-priority request (by default, the one that arrived last), frees all of its KV cache, and puts it back at the front of the waiting queue. When memory frees up, it’s re-admitted and its cache is rebuilt by running prefill again over its prompt and everything it had generated so far. This is recomputation, and V1 uses it because prefill is fast and compute-bound; the older alternative, swapping the cache out to CPU memory over PCIe, is often slower than just recomputing it.

Preemption is the safety valve that lets the scheduler be optimistic. Admitting aggressively keeps the batch big and throughput high, at the risk of occasionally throwing work away. Admitting conservatively never wastes work but leaves memory idle. Where you sit between those is another dial that trades throughput against tail latency.

Part 4: PagedAttention and Prefix Caching

Why Contiguous Caches Waste Memory

Part 2 ended with the central tension: the batch can only be as big as the KV cache allows. So how well the cache uses its memory directly sets the throughput. And before vLLM, it used memory very badly.

Serving systems at the time stored each request’s KV cache as one contiguous tensor, the way you’d naturally write it. But you don’t know how long a request’s answer will be when you allocate it, so you reserve space for the maximum possible length up front. The vLLM paper has a figure that shows exactly where that goes wrong:


Contiguous allocation reserves the maximum length per request. Most of it is never touched, and the gaps between allocations can’t be used either. Figure: Kwon et al. (2023), arXiv:2309.06180, CC BY 4.0

There are three kinds of waste in that picture. Reserved slots will be used eventually, but they sit empty for most of the request’s life. Internal fragmentation is space reserved for a maximum length the request never reaches: request A reserved 2,048 slots and finished after 10. External fragmentation is the gaps between allocations, too small or awkwardly placed for the next request. The vLLM authors measured that in the systems of the time, only 20.4% to 38.2% of the KV cache memory actually held token states. The rest was waste, and every wasted gigabyte was users who couldn’t be batched.

Paging the KV Cache

The fix is one of my favourite ideas in systems, because it’s borrowed straight from an operating system. Your OS faces the same problem with process memory: processes don’t know how much they’ll need, and contiguous physical allocations fragment. The answer, decades old, is virtual memory with paging: give each process a contiguous logical address space, back it with fixed-size physical pages scattered anywhere in RAM, and keep a page table that maps one to the other.

PagedAttention does exactly this for the KV cache. The cache is divided into fixed-size blocks, 16 tokens each by default in vLLM. For Llama 3 8B, one block holds 16 tokens × 128 KiB = 2 MiB of cache across all the layers. Each request has a block table that maps its logical blocks (tokens 0 to 15, 16 to 31, and so on) to physical blocks anywhere in GPU memory:


Logical blocks are contiguous; physical blocks are wherever there was room. The block table, like a page table, maps one to the other. Figure: Kwon et al. (2023), arXiv:2309.06180, CC BY 4.0

Blocks are allocated on demand, one at a time, as a request’s tokens fill them. Nothing is reserved for a future that may never come, so internal fragmentation is limited to the unfilled tail of each request’s last block (at most 15 tokens), and since all blocks are the same size, external fragmentation disappears entirely. vLLM’s waste dropped to under 4%.

The cost: attention can no longer read a request’s keys and values as one contiguous array. The PagedAttention kernel walks the block table and gathers keys and values block by block. That indirection made the attention kernel itself 20 to 26% slower than the best contiguous kernel of the time, in the paper’s own measurements. But attention is only part of each step, and the memory saved goes straight into bigger batches. The paper’s first figure shows what that buys:


Throughput climbs with batch size until memory runs out. Wasteful allocation hit that wall at batch 8; paging pushed it to about 40, roughly tripling throughput on the same GPU. Figure: Kwon et al. (2023), arXiv:2309.06180, CC BY 4.0

Read the bottom chart first: throughput rises steadily with batch size, which is the weight-sharing effect from Part 2, measured. Then the top: memory fills as the batch grows, and the earlier systems ran out at a batch of about 8. The model and the GPU didn’t change. How the cache was managed tripled the throughput. Across their benchmarks the authors reported 2 to 4 times the throughput of FasterTransformer and Orca at the same latency. A 20% slower kernel, a 3x faster system. Tradeoffs, as always.

Sharing Blocks: Prefix Caching

Paging has a second gift, and it’s the same one it gives operating systems: once memory is a table of pointers, two requests can point at the same physical block. A lot of real traffic shares long prefixes. Every request to a product carries the same system prompt. Every turn of a chat carries the whole conversation so far. Few-shot prompts repeat the same examples:


Two requests, one shared prefix. Its keys and values are identical in both, so they only need to be computed and stored once. Figure: Kwon et al. (2023), arXiv:2309.06180, CC BY 4.0

Because of causal attention, the keys and values of a shared prefix are identical in every request that starts with it. So vLLM’s automatic prefix caching gives every full block a hash computed from its tokens and the hash of the block before it, so a hash identifies the entire prefix up to that block, not just its own 16 tokens. When a new request arrives, the KV cache manager hashes its prompt block by block and looks each one up. Every hit is a block that doesn’t need to be computed at all. When a request finishes, its blocks aren’t wiped; they go onto a free list, still holding their contents, evicted least-recently-used first only when memory is actually needed. A popular system prompt effectively lives in the cache permanently.

The payoff is in prefill, and it can be huge. Turn five of a chat might carry 4,000 tokens of history and 100 new ones. With a cache hit, prefill processes 100 tokens instead of 4,100, and TTFT drops from tens of milliseconds to a few. SGLang, another popular engine, takes the same idea further with RadixAttention, which keeps all cached prefixes in a radix tree. When the same blocks are shared by several requests that diverge (parallel sampling, beam search), PagedAttention uses copy-on-write: the shared block is copied only when one of them needs to write into it, exactly as fork() does for process memory.

There’s one catch, and it’ll matter a lot in Part 6: a prefix cache only helps if the request lands on the GPU that holds the cache.

Part 5: Beyond One GPU

How Big Is Too Big

Everything so far assumed the model fits on one GPU. Often it doesn’t. Llama 3 70B in BF16 is about 141 GB of weights, and an H100 has 80. Llama 3.1 405B is about 810 GB, more than an entire eight-GPU H100 server’s 640 GB. And even when a model technically fits, it might leave no room for KV cache, which as we’ve seen means no batch, which means no throughput.

So the model has to be split. There are four basic ways to do it, and they split different things:


Tensor parallelism splits each layer, pipeline parallelism splits the stack of layers, data parallelism copies the model and splits the requests, expert parallelism splits a mixture-of-experts layer’s experts.

Tensor Parallelism

Tensor parallelism (TP) splits every weight matrix across GPUs, so each GPU holds a slice of every layer. The standard recipe is from NVIDIA’s Megatron-LM paper, and it’s elegant. In the MLP, the first matrix is split by columns: each GPU computes its own slice of the hidden activations, with no communication needed, because the activation function works element by element. The second matrix is split by rows, matching that slice, so each GPU produces a partial result for the full output. Summing the partial results across GPUs, an all-reduce, gives the right answer:


Column split, then row split, then one sum. Attention splits the same way, by heads.

Attention splits even more naturally, by heads: with 8 key-value heads and TP of 8, each GPU handles one KV head and its query heads, and holds only that head’s slice of the KV cache. That’s a nice property, because the KV cache is split too, not copied. It also sets a soft limit: TP beyond the number of KV heads means duplicating KV heads across GPUs.

For decode, tensor parallelism does something no other split does: it makes each step faster. Decode is memory-bound, and with TP each GPU reads only its share of the weights. Llama 3 70B at batch 1 on four H100s: each GPU reads about 35 GB per step, around 10.5 ms. On eight: 17.6 GB, around 5.3 ms. The cost is communication: two all-reduces per layer (one after attention, one after the MLP), so 160 per step for an 80-layer model. Each is small, but each has a latency of microseconds that you pay 160 times, every step. That’s why TP needs the fastest link available, NVLink at 900 GB/s between the GPUs of one server, and why it almost always stays inside a single server.

Pipeline Parallelism

Pipeline parallelism (PP) splits the model the other way: by layers. GPU 0 gets layers 1 to 40, GPU 1 gets 41 to 80, and the activations flow from one stage to the next. The traffic between stages is tiny, just one vector per token at each boundary (for a batch of 64 on Llama 3 70B, about 1 MB), so stages can sit on different servers connected by ordinary InfiniBand or Ethernet.

The catch is that one request’s step has to pass through every stage in turn, so PP doesn’t make a single request any faster. While stage 2 works, stage 1 would sit idle, unless there’s another batch to work on. So pipelines are kept busy by having several micro-batches in flight at once, each at a different stage, and the idle gaps at the start and end are called bubbles. Pipeline parallelism is how you go beyond one server: Llama 3.1 405B in BF16 can run as TP 8 inside each of two servers, with PP 2 between them. (Or you quantise the weights to FP8, about 405 GB, and fit it in one server. Another tradeoff.)

Data Parallelism and Expert Parallelism

Data parallelism (DP) is the simplest of all: run complete copies of the model, called replicas, and send each request to one of them. No communication between replicas, and throughput scales almost perfectly with the number of copies. The price is that every copy needs all the weights, which is why DP is usually layered on top of the others: four replicas, each spread over two GPUs with TP, is eight GPUs. DP is also where the next part of this post begins, because something has to decide which replica gets each request.

Expert parallelism (EP) exists because of mixture-of-experts (MoE) models. In an MoE layer, the single big MLP is replaced by many smaller “expert” MLPs and a router that sends each token to just a few of them. DeepSeek-V3, for example, has 671 billion parameters but uses only about 37 billion for any one token: each MoE layer has 256 routed experts, plus one shared expert, and each token visits 8 of the routed ones. EP spreads the experts over GPUs, so each GPU holds a few experts and every token’s vector has to be sent to whichever GPUs hold its chosen experts and back again, an all-to-all exchange in every MoE layer. Its failure mode is load imbalance: if one expert is popular for the current batch, its GPU becomes the bottleneck while the others wait.

MoE models also show how these choices interact with the KV cache. DeepSeek’s multi-head latent attention stores one compressed latent per token instead of separate heads, so splitting attention by heads with TP would just copy that cache onto every GPU. vLLM’s answer is to run attention with data parallelism (each GPU owns different requests and their caches) and the expert layers with expert parallelism across the same GPUs.

Choosing a Layout

In vLLM, each of these is a flag, and they compose:

The rules of thumb follow from the physics. Use TP inside a server, as little as fits: enough to hold the weights with plenty of room left for KV cache, and more if you need lower per-token latency, but every extra GPU adds all-reduce cost. Use PP only to cross server boundaries. Use EP for MoE layers. And then use DP to multiply the whole arrangement until you have enough throughput. A 70B model on TP 4 with two replicas, or on TP 8 with one, uses the same eight GPUs, but the first gives more total throughput and the second gives faster individual streams. Which is better depends on your latency targets, which is the theme of Part 7.

Part 6: Many Replicas: Splitting the Traffic

Why Round Robin Isn’t Enough

Once you have more than one replica, every incoming request needs a destination. For an ordinary web service this is a solved problem: put a load balancer in front and use round robin, sending request 1 to replica 1, request 2 to replica 2, and so on. It’s simple, stateless and perfectly fair by count. Kubernetes’ default service routing behaves much the same way.

For LLMs it’s surprisingly bad, for two reasons we’ve already met. First, requests aren’t equal. A “hi” with a 10-token answer and a 30-page document with a 2,000-token summary differ in cost by a factor of a thousand or more, and nobody knows the answer length in advance. Round robin happily sends three monsters in a row to the same replica, whose queue explodes while its neighbours idle. Second, replicas aren’t equal either. Each one holds a different KV cache, full of prefixes from the requests it happened to serve. Sending a conversation’s fifth turn to a replica that’s never seen it throws away 4,000 tokens of cached prefill.

So LLM routing has to know about load and about cache contents. Here’s the same request under three policies:


Round robin ignores both load and cache. Least-loaded fixes load but throws away the cache. Cache-aware routing weighs both.

Load-Aware Routing

The first improvement is to look at load. vLLM exports live metrics for each replica, including how many requests are running, how many are waiting and how full its KV cache is, and a router can use them. The simplest load-aware policy is least outstanding requests: send each request to the replica with the fewest in flight. Better ones score replicas on queue length and KV cache usage, or even estimate each request’s cost from its prompt length.

There’s a beautiful result from probability that matters here, because checking every replica’s load on every request gets expensive with hundreds of replicas and the information is always slightly stale. The power of two choices: pick two replicas at random and send the request to the less loaded one. If you throw n balls into n bins completely at random, the fullest bin ends up with about log n / log log n balls. With two random choices per ball, it drops to about log log n / log 2, an exponential improvement from looking at just one extra bin. Most of the benefit of perfect information, for almost none of the cost.

Cache-Aware Routing

The next step is to route for the cache. The crudest version is session affinity: hash the conversation ID and always send a conversation to the same replica. That keeps multi-turn chats on warm caches but knows nothing about shared system prompts across users, and it creates hot spots when one session is heavy. Better is to hash the prompt prefix itself with consistent hashing, so requests that start the same way go to the same replica, usually with a bound on how much load any one replica will accept.

The most precise routers model what each replica actually has in its cache. SGLang’s router keeps an approximate radix tree of the prefixes it has sent to each worker. llm-d (a Kubernetes-native project built around vLLM, and the subject of a later series here) has replicas publish events whenever they store or evict KV blocks, so the scheduler knows exactly which blocks live where. NVIDIA’s Dynamo has a KV-aware router in the same spirit, and vLLM’s own production stack router offers round robin, session-based and prefix-aware modes.

None of them route on cache alone, because that’s a trap: if every request with a popular system prompt goes to the replica that cached it first, that replica melts while the others idle. Cache locality and load balance pull against each other, and real routers combine them into a score, something like the number of prefix tokens already cached minus a penalty for queue depth and KV usage, then pick the best. Tuning that balance is most of what Part VII of the series is about.

Splitting One Request: Disaggregated Prefill and Decode

So far, each request lives on one replica from start to finish. But remember Part 2: prefill and decode want completely different things from the hardware. Prefill is compute-bound and bursty; decode is memory-bound and steady. Putting them on the same GPU means they constantly interfere (that’s what chunked prefill is managing), and you have to provision the GPU for both.

Disaggregated serving gives them separate GPUs. A request’s prefill runs on a prefill instance, which then ships the finished KV cache over a fast network to a decode instance, which generates the rest of the answer. vLLM’s docs show the flow:


One request, two GPUs: prefill on one instance, decode on another, with the KV cache handed across in between. Diagram: vLLM project, Apache 2.0

The benefit is that each pool can be sized and tuned for its own job, and the two latencies stop fighting: TTFT is set by the prefill pool, TPOT by the decode pool, and each can be scaled independently. The DistServe paper reported serving up to 7.4 times more requests, or meeting 12.6 times tighter latency targets, compared to colocated systems. The cost is the transfer. Llama 3 70B’s cache for a 4K-token prompt is about 1.3 GB, which has to cross the network for every request, so disaggregation needs RDMA-class interconnects and pays off most when prompts are long and latency targets are strict. It’s the last part of the series, because it builds on everything before it.

Part 7: Running at Maximum Utilisation

Throughput vs Latency: Choosing Where to Stand

We now have every piece, so let’s go back to the question from the start: how do you get the most out of a GPU-hour? The answer is to run the biggest batch your latency targets allow, and no bigger. Here’s that tradeoff for decode, from the same simple model as Part 2:


Throughput saturates; latency doesn’t. The operating point is the biggest batch that still meets the target.

Throughput climbs steeply at first and then flattens, because every request added to the batch brings its own KV cache to read, so the step time grows linearly while the shared weight read stops mattering. TPOT rises in a straight line. If your target is a TPOT of 15 ms, the best you can do is a batch of about 127, at about 8,500 tokens per second. Going from 127 to 240 would buy only 18% more throughput while making every user’s stream 60% slower. And going below 127 just throws throughput away.

That’s what “maximum utilisation” really means for an LLM server. Not 100% busy at any cost, but as close as possible to the latency target without crossing it. Every technique in this post moves the curves. PagedAttention raises how far right you can go before memory runs out. Prefix caching and chunked prefill keep the steps short and the batch full. Smaller KV caches (GQA, FP8 KV) flatten the TPOT line, so the target is crossed at a bigger batch.

What a GPU-Hour Buys

Now put a price on it. At $3 per GPU-hour, here’s what a million output tokens costs at different batch sizes:


The hourly price never changes. Only how many tokens you get out of each hour does.

Same GPU, same model, same hourly bill, and the cost per token differs by a factor of fifty. This is why inference companies obsess over batch sizes and cache layouts, and why the numbers on public price lists for open models are as low as they are.

Now we can do the arithmetic I promised at the start. Suppose a service gets 100 requests per second, each with a 1,000-token prompt and a 300-token answer, on Llama 3 8B.

Served one request at a time, each request takes a few tens of milliseconds of prefill and then 300 decode steps of about 4.9 ms: roughly 1.5 seconds per request, so each GPU completes about 0.65 requests per second. A hundred requests a second needs about 150 GPUs, roughly $450 an hour.

Served well, it looks completely different, and the tool for estimating it is Little’s law from the CUDA post: the number of things in a system equals their arrival rate times how long each one stays. With a TPOT of 15 ms, each answer takes 300 × 15 ms = 4.5 seconds, so at 100 arrivals a second there are about 100 × 4.5 = 450 requests decoding at any moment. At about 127 per GPU, decode needs between three and four GPUs. Prefill adds 100,000 prompt tokens a second at 16 GFLOPs each, 1.6 PFLOP/s of compute-bound work, which at a realistic 60% of the H100’s peak is close to three more GPUs’ worth (and with chunked prefill it shares the same GPUs as decode rather than needing separate ones). That’s about seven GPUs in all, around $21 an hour. These are back-of-the-envelope numbers, and a real deployment needs headroom for traffic bursts. But the ratio is the point: twenty times less hardware for the same traffic, from software alone.

The Other Levers

Batching and memory management are the big two, but there are more dials, and production systems turn all of them.

CUDA graphs attack overhead. A decode step for a small model takes only a few milliseconds, and it’s made of hundreds of small kernels. Launching each one from Python costs microseconds of CPU time, which adds up to a noticeable fraction of the step. vLLM captures each step’s whole sequence of kernels as a CUDA graph for common batch sizes and replays it with a single launch (the same trick from the CUDA post). V1 also overlaps the scheduler’s CPU work for the next step with the GPU’s work on the current one.

Quantisation attacks the weight wall directly. Decode time is weight bytes over bandwidth, so storing weights in FP8 instead of BF16 halves the bytes: Llama 3 8B drops from 16 GB to 8 GB, and the single-user bound from 4.9 ms per token to about 2.4 ms. The H100’s FP8 tensor cores also double peak compute for prefill. Quantising the KV cache to FP8 halves its size too, which means twice as many requests fit. The cost is a little accuracy, which I covered in the quantisation post.

Speculative decoding turns decode’s idleness into speed. A small, fast draft model (or a cheap heuristic, or extra prediction heads) guesses the next few tokens, and the big model checks all of them in one forward pass. Because decode is memory-bound, checking five tokens costs about the same as generating one: the weights are read once either way. Every correct guess is a token for free. It helps most at small batch sizes, where the GPU has lots of idle compute to spend; at large batches the compute is already being used, and the benefit shrinks.

And finally, autoscaling handles the fact that traffic isn’t constant. It rises in the daytime and falls at night, and an idle replica still costs its full price. Scaling replicas up and down on the right signals (queue length, KV cache usage, TTFT, not CPU usage, which says almost nothing about a GPU server) is how a fleet’s utilisation stays high around the clock. It’s harder than it sounds, because loading a 140 GB model onto a fresh GPU takes minutes, and that’s the sort of problem llm-d exists for.

Putting It Together

Follow a request through the whole stack and the picture fits together. It arrives at an API server, gets tokenised, and maybe routed by a cache-aware router to the replica most likely to hold its prefix. The engine core’s scheduler admits it when the KV cache manager can find blocks for it, reusing any cached prefix blocks it can. Its prefill, a compute-bound burst, is chunked and mixed into steps alongside everyone else’s decode tokens, which are memory-bound and ride along almost for free. Once it’s decoding, it gets one token per step in a continuous batch that requests are constantly joining and leaving, its cache growing one paged block at a time. If the model is too big for one GPU, every one of those steps is spread over several, with tensor parallelism inside a server and pipeline or expert parallelism beyond. When it finishes, its blocks go back to the free list, still warm for the next request that shares its prefix.

And it all comes back to two facts from Part 2. Decode is memory-bound, so it’s only efficient when many requests share each read of the weights. And every request’s KV cache takes memory that can’t be shared, so memory caps the batch. Continuous batching keeps the batch full. PagedAttention and prefix caching make the memory go further. Parallelism adds memory and bandwidth. Routing makes sure caches get reused. And the throughput-latency curve tells you how far to push it all.

Why This Is Important

Training gets the headlines, but think about where the GPU-hours actually go. A model is trained once. It’s served every time anyone uses it, for as long as it exists. For any model that people genuinely use, the hardware spent answering them quickly dwarfs the hardware spent creating it. And the tokens per user are going up, not down: reasoning models think in thousands of hidden tokens before they answer, and agents call the model again and again inside a single task. Every one of those tokens goes through the loop in this post.

That’s why inference engineering has turned into a discipline of its own, and why I wanted to start this series now. The people doing it aren’t mainly researching new models. They’re doing exactly what this post did: working out whether a workload is compute-bound or memory-bound, how much KV cache a context length costs, which batching and parallelism settings hit a latency target, and how many GPUs that means. A 20× difference in hardware for the same traffic, from software alone, is the kind of number that decides whether a product makes money.

It’s also why I keep seeing roles like the forward deployed engineer next to it: engineers who sit with a customer and get models running on their hardware and their traffic. When a customer asks why their time to first token spiked at lunchtime, or how many H100s their launch will need, the answer is the arithmetic from this post: prompt lengths, Little’s law, KV memory per user, and where on the throughput-latency curve they’ve decided to stand.

For me, the part that makes all this worth learning properly is that none of it is arbitrary. My CUDA post ended on kernels waiting for memory, and here the same fact decides how much a chatbot costs to run. PagedAttention is virtual memory, prefix caching is copy-on-write, continuous batching is a scheduler choosing at every step instead of every job, and the power of two choices is a 1990s probability result. If you understand the hardware and know a bit of systems history, most of this field reads like something you could have derived yourself. That’s the level I want this series to reach.

This one ended up being the longest map I’ve drawn on this blog, and that was deliberate: every later part zooms into one region of it.
