The Compiler Map: From Source Code to GPU, and Where AI Compilers Fit

2026-10-17

Your model is a list of tensor operations. Your GPU runs kernels. Everything in between is a compiler.


Cover photo: Rob Bulmahn via Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0

In my CUDA post, Squeezing the SM, I wrote kernels by hand: coalesced loads, shared memory tiles, tensor core fragments, all of it. Then in my inference posts, I kept running into code that nobody writes by hand. vLLM spends a minute compiling the model before it serves a single request. Its decode steps run as captured CUDA graphs. Its matmuls come from cuBLAS, its attention from FlashAttention, and most of the remaining kernels are generated on the fly by torch.compile. I’d been treating all of that as a black box, and it started to bother me. Who actually writes the kernels nobody writes?

The answer is a whole stack of compilers, and once I started reading about it I realised it connects almost everything on this blog. The classic compiler that turns C into machine code. LLVM, which a huge fraction of the world’s compilers are built on. MLIR, the newer infrastructure built for tensors. AI compilers like torch.compile, XLA and TVM that sit on top of all of it. And at the very bottom, back ends that have to know exactly how a chip’s caches and memory ordering work.

So this post is a map of that whole stack, from a PyTorch model down to the SASS instructions that run on the SMs, and I’ve tried to explain how and why each layer works rather than just naming it. To keep myself honest, I’ve used real output wherever I could. Every listing of tokens, syntax trees, LLVM IR, x86 assembly, PTX, SASS, FX graphs, guards, Inductor IR and generated kernels below was produced on my own laptop, with clang 21, CUDA 11.8 and PyTorch 2.12, and I’ll say so clearly in the one place where I couldn’t. Fair warning: it’s long. Here’s the whole map in one picture; the rest of the post walks it from the bottom layer of classic compilers up to the AI compilers, and then back down to the hardware:


Three layers of compiler between your model and the silicon. The top one knows about tensors, the middle one about tiles, and the bottom one about instructions.

Part 1: What a Compiler Actually Does

Keep the Meaning, Make It Fast

A compiler has exactly two jobs, and they pull against each other. It must preserve the meaning of your program, and within that constraint, it should make the program as cheap as possible to run on a particular machine.

“Preserve the meaning” is more precise than it sounds. A C compiler doesn’t promise to execute your statements in order, or to keep your variables in memory, or even to perform every operation you wrote. It promises only that the program’s observable behaviour (what it reads and writes outside itself, its output, its return value) is what the language standard says it should be. This is called the as-if rule: the compiler may do anything at all, as long as the result behaves as if it had executed your code literally. Everything clever a compiler does lives inside that freedom.

The language definition also matters at the edges. In C, a signed integer overflow is undefined behaviour: the standard says nothing about what happens, so the compiler is allowed to assume it never does. That sounds like a technicality, but it’s a real source of optimisation, and we’ll see it pay off in the first listing below. Rules like that are the contract between you and the compiler: you promise not to do certain things, and in exchange it can assume you didn’t.

The classic way to organise the work is into three phases, and Chris Lattner, who created LLVM, drew it this way in his chapter of The Architecture of Open Source Applications:


The front end understands the language, the optimiser improves the program, the back end understands the machine. Figure: Chris Lattner, The Architecture of Open Source Applications (2011), CC BY 3.0

The front end knows the source language and nothing about the machine. The back end knows the machine and nothing about the language. In between, the optimiser works on a neutral representation of the program that neither side owns. The separation is the whole point: each phase can be written, tested and replaced on its own, and as Part 2 shows, it’s also what lets one optimiser serve many languages and many chips. To make this concrete, I’ll follow one tiny C function through every phase:

Lexing: Characters Into Words

To the compiler, a source file starts as a stream of characters. The first job, lexing (or tokenising), groups those characters into tokens, the words of the language: keywords, identifiers, numbers, operators and punctuation. Clang will show you its token stream if you ask (-Xclang -dump-tokens), and for our function it begins exactly as you’d expect:

A lexer is essentially a finite state machine: it reads one character at a time and moves between states like “inside an identifier” or “inside a number” until a character arrives that can’t continue the current token. One rule decides all the ambiguous cases: maximal munch, always take the longest token that fits. That’s why i++ is one increment operator and not two plus signs, and why a>>b is a shift rather than two greater-than signs. Whitespace and comments are discarded here, apart from a couple of flags like “this token started a line” that later stages occasionally need for diagnostics. After this stage, the program is a flat list of words, with no idea yet how they relate to each other.

Parsing: Words Into Structure

Parsing turns that flat list into a tree that captures the program’s structure, according to the language’s grammar: a set of rules like “a for statement is the keyword for, a parenthesised header with three parts, and then a statement”. The grammar also encodes precedence: in s += a[i] * a[i], the multiplication must bind more tightly than the compound assignment, so the tree has the multiplication underneath the +=, not beside it.

Clang uses a hand-written recursive descent parser: roughly one function per grammar rule, each calling the others as it recognises nested constructs (a ParseForStatement that calls ParseExpression for the condition, and so on). It’s not the textbook approach of generating a parser from a grammar file, but it gives much better error messages and handles C++’s notoriously context-dependent grammar, which is why most production compilers do it this way. The result is an abstract syntax tree (AST). “Abstract” because it drops pure syntax, like parentheses and semicolons, that only existed to make the structure unambiguous. Here’s part of clang’s real AST for our function (-Xclang -ast-dump), trimmed of memory addresses:

Read it top down and it’s the function’s structure exactly: a declaration of s initialised to 0, then a for loop whose condition is i < n, whose increment is i++, and whose body is a += with s on the left and a multiplication of two array subscripts on the right.

Semantic Analysis: Structure Into Meaning

Notice that the tree above carries more than structure. Every node has a type, and there are nodes that don’t appear anywhere in the source, like ImplicitCastExpr <LValueToRValue>. That’s the work of semantic analysis, the front end’s last and most language-specific job.

It does three main things. It resolves every name to its declaration, using a symbol table that tracks which names are visible in each scope; that’s how the i in the condition is linked to the i declared in the loop header, and why i doesn’t exist after the loop. It type checks every expression: a[i] is only legal because a is a pointer and i is an integer, and the result has type int. And it makes the language’s implicit rules explicit. LValueToRValue is C’s rule that when you use a variable’s name in an expression, you mean “the value stored there”, not “the storage location”; the AST spells that out as a conversion node, so later stages don’t have to know C’s rules. If you’ve ever seen “use of undeclared identifier” or “incompatible pointer types”, it came from here. After this point, the program’s meaning is fully pinned down.

Into the IR

The front end’s final step is to translate the checked AST into the optimiser’s language, an intermediate representation (IR). For clang that’s LLVM IR, and before reading it, it helps to know what kind of language it is. LLVM IR looks like assembly for an imaginary machine with three useful properties. It’s typed: every value has a type like i32 (a 32-bit integer) or ptr. It has an unlimited supply of registers: any value can be given a new name (%5, %add), and worrying about how many registers the real machine has is postponed to the very end. And control flow is explicit: the code is divided into basic blocks, straight-line sequences of instructions that always run start to finish, each ending in a branch to other blocks. The blocks and the branches between them form the function’s control-flow graph.

At -O0, with no optimisation, clang’s translation is completely literal. Here’s the loop body:

Every C variable, even the parameters, has been given a slot on the stack (created by alloca instructions at the top of the function, not shown), and every use is a load from memory and every assignment a store. a[i] is loaded twice, because the source mentions it twice. sext sign-extends i to 64 bits, because addresses are 64-bit. getelementptr (“GEP”) is LLVM’s address arithmetic instruction: it computes a + i × 4 from the element type, without touching memory. And mul nsw is a multiply with the no signed wrap flag: the front end is passing on C’s promise that signed overflow never happens, so later passes are allowed to assume it. It’s all correct, and deliberately naive. The front end’s job is to be simple and right, and leave speed to the optimiser.

SSA: One Definition per Value

The first big thing the optimiser does is lift those variables out of memory into static single assignment (SSA) form. The rule is in the name: every value is assigned exactly once, in exactly one place. A variable like s that changes on every iteration can’t follow that rule directly, so SSA introduces phi nodes at the points where control-flow paths merge. A phi says “my value is this if we arrived from that block, or that if we arrived from the other block”. Here’s the loop at -O1, which is SSA with the stack slots gone:

Drawn as a graph, the structure is easier to see:


Every value is defined once. The phis at the top of the loop are how a changing variable fits the single-assignment rule.

Why go to this trouble? Because in SSA form, the question “where did this value come from?” always has exactly one answer, and almost every optimisation is built on that question. Is this value a constant? Look at its single definition. Are these two loads the same? Check whether they have the same address and nothing in between could have written to it. Is this value used anywhere? Check whether its definition has any users; if not, delete it. In the original form, where s was a memory location written in several places, each of those questions needed a complicated analysis of every path through the function. In SSA they’re cheap, and so the optimiser can afford to ask them constantly.

Compare the two listings and you can see several optimisations already applied. The two loads of a[i] became one, because in SSA it’s obvious they read the same address with no store in between. The 32-bit i, which needed a sign extension every iteration, became a 64-bit counter (%9) that can be used as an address directly. The nsw promise from the front end made that legal: since i can’t overflow, widening it can’t change the result. The loop condition changed from i < n to i + 1 == n, which is equivalent here because the entry block already checked that n is positive. And the !tbaa tag attached to the load is type-based alias analysis information, recording that this load reads an int, so the optimiser may assume it can’t be changed by a store of some unrelated type. A loop body of about twenty-five instructions, most of them memory traffic, became seven plus two phi nodes.

Passes, One Small Change at a Time

Each of those improvements was made by a separate pass: a small transformation that takes valid IR and returns valid IR that does the same thing more cheaply. LLVM will tell you which passes actually changed a function (-mllvm -print-changed). For square_sum at -O1, the passes that made changes included:

  • SROA (scalar replacement of aggregates), which promoted the stack slots to SSA values and built the phi nodes. This is the single most important early pass; everything after it works on registers instead of memory.
  • EarlyCSE (common subexpression elimination), which noticed the two identical loads of a[i] and deleted one.
  • InstCombine, a huge collection of small algebraic simplifications (peephole rules like x × 1 → x, or (a + b) − b → a), which ran several times as other passes exposed new opportunities.
  • SimplifyCFG, which merges blocks, removes branches that can never be taken, and generally tidies the control-flow graph.
  • LoopRotate, which turns a while-shaped loop (check the condition, then run the body) into a do-while shape guarded by one check before the loop. That’s where the separate “preheader” block, which runs exactly once, comes from.
  • IndVarSimplify, which rewrites induction variables, the counters that step through a loop. It’s what widened i to 64 bits and changed the exit test.

At -O1, the multiplication %8 = fmul float %2, %3 appears in the preheader block, which runs once, and the loop body only multiplies each element by %8. LLVM’s pass log shows that LICMPass ran immediately after LoopRotatePass. That ordering isn’t an accident: LoopRotate creates the preheader, and LICM needs a preheader to have somewhere to put the code it hoists.

That’s the general shape of an optimiser: a pipeline of a few dozen kinds of pass, many run several times, each relying on what earlier passes set up. Choosing the order is genuinely hard (it’s known as the phase-ordering problem), because a pass can create opportunities for one pass and destroy them for another, and the -O1, -O2 and -O3 levels are essentially different pre-tuned orderings of different sets of passes. A full -O3 pipeline runs well over a hundred passes. None of them is clever on its own; the power is in the composition.

Vectorisation: Doing Eight at Once

At -O3, targeting a CPU with AVX2 (256-bit vector registers), the loop transforms completely:

<8 x i32> is a vector of eight 32-bit integers, which fits exactly in one 256-bit register, so one instruction now does the work of eight. This is the loop vectoriser, and it has to answer two questions before it changes anything.

The first is legality: is it actually allowed? Processing eight iterations at once is only correct if they don’t depend on each other. Here they don’t, except through s, and s is a reduction, a running sum. The vectoriser handles reductions by keeping eight partial sums, one per vector lane, and adding them together after the loop. That reorders the additions, which is fine for integers, because integer addition is associative. For floating-point it wouldn’t be: (a + b) + c and a + (b + c) can round differently, so a float sum is only vectorised this way if you allow reassociation with fast-math flags. Memory is the other legality question. In the scale function above, the compiler can’t know whether out and in point at overlapping memory, and if they did, writing eight outputs at once could overwrite inputs the loop hasn’t read yet. So at -O3 it adds a runtime check. This is the real code it generated before the vector loop:

If the arrays might overlap, or there are too few elements, it takes the original scalar loop; otherwise, the vector one. When I marked the pointers restrict (C’s way of promising they never overlap), the check disappeared from the output entirely. That’s the as-if rule and undefined behaviour working together again: a promise from the programmer turns into a check the compiler no longer needs.

The second question is profitability: is it actually faster? The vectoriser consults a cost model of the target CPU, an estimate of how expensive each instruction is, and compares the scalar and vector loops. It also picks how many vector operations to keep in flight at once. Here it chose to interleave by four: the real loop body has four independent vector accumulators (%16 to %19 in the full listing), processing 32 elements per trip, and only combines them at the very end. That’s exactly the multiple-accumulators trick from the instruction-level parallelism chapter of my CUDA post: four independent chains of additions can overlap in the CPU’s pipeline, where a single chain would make every addition wait for the one before. The compiler found it on its own. Finally, because n isn’t usually a multiple of 32, the vectoriser also generates an epilogue, a smaller vector loop and then a scalar loop that mop up the remaining few elements; you can see them in the output as blocks named vec.epilog and for.body.prol.

The Back End: IR Into Instructions

The last phase knows the machine, and it has three main jobs.

Instruction selection maps IR operations onto the machine’s real instructions. LLVM’s classic selector, SelectionDAG, builds a graph of the operations in each basic block, then legalises it (rewriting anything the target can’t do directly, like a 64-bit multiply on a 32-bit chip, into things it can), and then matches patterns in the graph against a table of the target’s instructions. The pattern tables are generated from instruction descriptions written in a language called TableGen, which I’ll come back to in Part 7. A newer selector, GlobalISel, does the same job in a different way and is gradually taking over on some targets.

Register allocation fits the program’s unlimited supply of SSA values into the machine’s real registers: sixteen general-purpose registers and sixteen vector registers on x86-64. The core idea is the live range: the stretch of the program from where a value is defined to its last use. Two values whose live ranges overlap need different registers at the same time; two that don’t can share one. If at some point more values are live than there are registers, some must be spilled: stored to memory and reloaded later, which is slow. LLVM’s main allocator is a “greedy” one that assigns the most important live ranges first and splits or spills the rest. Here’s the real scalar -O1 loop for x86 and how the values ended up in registers:


Each bar is a live range. Overlapping ranges get different registers, and nothing here comes close to running out.

Some of those choices weren’t the allocator’s to make. On Windows, the x64 calling convention says a function’s first two integer arguments arrive in rcx and rdx, and an integer result must be returned in eax. So a and n start where the convention put them, and the allocator chose to compute s directly in eax so that the return needs no extra move. That’s a nice small example of how much the back end has to know about the platform, not just the chip.

Finally, instruction scheduling orders instructions so that slow ones (loads, multiplies) start early and their results are ready just in time, using a model of the CPU’s pipeline, and the MC layer (machine code) encodes each instruction into bytes. Part 2: LLVM and the M × N Problem

One IR in the Middle

Here’s the problem LLVM was built to solve. Suppose you have M source languages and N target machines. If every compiler is written from scratch for one language and one machine, you need M × N compilers, and every optimisation from Part 1 has to be reimplemented in each of them. The three-phase design breaks that apart, as long as everybody agrees on the IR in the middle:


Front ends only need to produce LLVM IR; back ends only need to consume it. M × N compilers become M + N components. Figure: Chris Lattner, The Architecture of Open Source Applications (2011), CC BY 3.0

A new language only needs a front end that produces LLVM IR, and it immediately gets every optimisation and every back end. A new chip only needs a back end, and immediately runs every language. That’s why Clang, Rust, Swift, Julia and Zig all compile through LLVM. The idea of a shared IR wasn’t new; what made LLVM succeed was that its IR is a real, complete, documented language (the LLVM Language Reference specifies every instruction precisely), with three interchangeable forms: an in-memory data structure for passes to work on, the readable text form you’ve been looking at (.ll files), and a compact binary “bitcode” form (.bc). Because the IR is the contract, front-end and back-end authors never need to talk to each other.

The Same IR, Pointed at a GPU

The part that matters for this blog is that GPUs are just more back ends. LLVM has an NVPTX back end that produces NVIDIA’s PTX, and an AMDGPU back end that produces machine code for AMD’s GPUs. NVIDIA’s own nvcc also uses an LLVM-based optimiser internally (they call it NVVM). To see it, I wrote the fused kernel we’ll meet again in Part 5, y = relu(x · a + b), and compiled it two ways. Here’s the LLVM IR clang produced for an RTX 4060 (sm_89):

It’s the same IR language as the CPU version, which is the whole point of LLVM. The GPU-specific parts are small. The function is marked ptx_kernel, so it can be launched from the host rather than called. Thread and block indices are read with intrinsics, built-in functions the back end knows how to turn into special register reads. And though it isn’t visible in this tiny kernel, NVPTX uses LLVM’s address spaces to tell global, shared, local and constant memory apart, because they’re different hardware with different instructions.

One detail deserves a closer look: the contract flag on the multiply and the add. A fused multiply-add (FMA) computes x × a + b with a single rounding at the end, instead of rounding once after the multiply and again after the add. That’s faster and usually slightly more accurate, but it’s a different result, so under the as-if rule the compiler isn’t allowed to do it unless the source language permits it. CUDA permits it by default, and the front end records that permission on each operation with contract. In Part 7 we’ll see the exact line in the NVPTX back end that checks this flag.

What PTX Is, and Isn’t

As I covered in the CUDA post, PTX is a virtual instruction set. It looks like assembly, but it’s really another IR: NVIDIA’s stable, documented contract between compilers and their GPUs. It has an unlimited number of typed virtual registers (%f for floats, %r for integers, %rd for 64-bit values, %p for predicates), and it describes operations without committing to any particular chip’s encoding. That stability is the point: PTX written today will still run on a GPU released in five years, because the driver can compile it for that GPU when the program loads. A CUDA binary usually carries both: machine code for the GPUs it was built for, and the PTX as a fallback.

A few things in nvcc’s full PTX listing are worth decoding. Kernel arguments are read with ld.param, from a special parameter space. cvta.to.global converts a generic address (one that could point into any memory space) into a global one, so the load can use the faster global-memory instruction. And control flow uses predicates: setp.ge.s32 %p1, %r1, %r2 sets a one-bit predicate register to “is i ≥ n?”, and @%p1 bra $L__BB0_2 branches only if it’s true. Predicates matter on GPUs because whole warps execute together, and many short conditionals are cheaper to execute with predication than to branch around.

ptxas: The Real Back End

The real back end for NVIDIA GPUs is ptxas, NVIDIA’s closed-source assembler. It does the final instruction selection, register allocation and scheduling for one specific architecture, and produces SASS, the actual machine code. NVIDIA doesn’t document SASS in detail, partly because it changes with every generation and partly because the stability promise lives in PTX instead. cuobjdump will disassemble it, though. The kernel’s arguments never get loaded into registers at all: they sit in a constant bank, c[0x0][...], and instructions read them directly as operands. On this architecture the user parameters start at offset 0x160, so x is at 0x160, y at 0x168, a at 0x170, b at 0x174 and n at 0x178, exactly in declaration order. IMAD (integer multiply-add) computes the thread index in one instruction, the integer cousin of the FMA. IMAD.WIDE produces a 64-bit result, which is how a 32-bit index becomes a 64-bit address. The bounds check didn’t become a branch around the work, but a predicated exit: threads with i ≥ n simply stop. FMNMX with RZ, a register hard-wired to zero, is the ReLU. And ptxas -v reports the cost: ten registers, no spills, no stack. Sixteen instructions in all, a few of them housekeeping.

That’s the bottom of the map. Everything above this point, all the way up to PyTorch, exists to produce code like this, for much more complicated programs, automatically.

Part 3: Why Tensors Broke the Old Model

What LLVM Can’t See

If LLVM is so good, why isn’t it enough for machine learning? Because by the time a program reaches LLVM IR, the most useful information has already been thrown away.

Think about a matrix multiply. In a model, it’s one operation with a name: matmul(A, B). Lowered to LLVM IR, it becomes three nested loops of loads, multiply-adds and stores. Now consider what you’d want a compiler to do with a large matmul on a GPU. Tile it, so each block of the output is computed from blocks of the inputs that fit in shared memory. Map the innermost tile onto tensor core instructions. Lay out the inputs so the tiles load efficiently. Fuse a bias add and an activation onto the output. Every one of those depends on knowing that this loop nest is a matmul, with its particular algebraic structure. Recovering that from scalar IR is possible in principle (researchers spent years on polyhedral loop analysis frameworks that try), but it’s fragile and expensive, and LLVM’s optimiser doesn’t attempt it. The information existed at the top of the stack and was discarded on the way down.

There’s a second, bigger problem. A model isn’t one function; it’s a graph of hundreds of operations, and as Part 5 will show, the biggest wins come from optimising across them: fusing a chain of operations into one kernel, and deciding which intermediate tensors never need to exist at all. LLVM optimises one function at a time, and never sees the graph that the functions came from.

MLIR: Many Levels, One Framework

The answer that much of the field has converged on is MLIR, the Multi-Level Intermediate Representation, created at Google by Chris Lattner and colleagues and now part of the LLVM project. Its central idea is that there shouldn’t be one IR but many, each capturing a different level of abstraction, all living inside one framework so they can be mixed in the same program and converted between.

The vocabulary is small. Everything in MLIR is an operation: a function, a loop, an addition, a matmul, even a module. Operations have typed operands and results, compile-time attributes, and can contain regions, nested blocks of more operations (that’s how a loop holds its body). Related operations and types are grouped into dialects: there’s an arith dialect for basic arithmetic, scf for structured control flow like for loops, memref for buffers in memory, tensor for immutable tensor values, linalg for linear algebra, vector for SIMD-style operations, gpu for kernels and thread indices, and an llvm dialect that mirrors LLVM IR one to one. Anyone can define a new dialect for their own domain, and Triton, XLA and IREE all have.

Here’s what a matmul looks like at the highest level, written by me in the linalg dialect for illustration:

It’s one operation, with the shapes in its types. A pass that wants to tile it doesn’t need to analyse any loops; it rewrites one linalg.matmul into a loop over smaller linalg.matmuls. Later passes lower the program step by step, a process called progressive lowering: tensors become memrefs (actual buffers in memory, a step called bufferization, which is also where the compiler decides which intermediate results can share storage), matmuls become scf.for loops of loads and multiply-adds, inner loops become vector operations sized for the hardware, and finally everything becomes the llvm dialect and then real LLVM IR:


Each level forgets something. MLIR lets each optimisation run before the thing it needs to know is forgotten.

The design principle is that each optimisation runs at the highest level where it still has the information it needs. Tiling and fusion happen while a matmul is still a matmul. Loop transformations happen on loops. Vectorisation happens once the shapes of the hardware are known. Nothing about LLVM is replaced: MLIR adds the levels above it that machine learning needed, and hands over to LLVM at the bottom. That’s why so much of the ML compiler world is now built on it, including OpenXLA’s StableHLO, IREE, the Triton compiler and Modular’s Mojo.

Triton’s Compiler, Read From the Source

Triton is the clearest real example, because its compiler is short and readable, and because it’s the compiler that torch.compile uses to generate GPU kernels. I read its NVIDIA back end in the Triton repository. A kernel goes through exactly five stages: TTIR (Triton IR, the kernel as you wrote it, operating on whole tiles of data), TTGIR (Triton GPU IR, where those tiles are assigned to threads and warps), LLIR (LLVM IR), PTX via LLVM’s NVPTX back end, and finally a cubin, produced by calling ptxas. The first two are MLIR dialects; the last three are the same path we followed in Part 2.

The key idea in TTGIR is the layout. A Triton program says “load this 64 × 64 tile”, but a GPU executes threads, so somebody has to decide which thread loads and holds which elements of the tile. TTGIR records that decision as an attribute attached to every tensor’s type. Here’s a real layout from Triton’s own test suite:

Read it as a recipe for distributing a 2D tile. Each thread holds one element at a time (sizePerThread = [1, 1]). The 32 threads of a warp are arranged as 32 rows and 1 column (threadsPerWarp = [32, 1]). Four warps are stacked along the rows. And order = [0, 1] says dimension 0 varies fastest when numbering threads. For a tile stored row by row in memory, that’s a terrible layout for loading: consecutive threads are a whole row apart, so a warp’s 32 loads hit 32 different cache lines. Now each thread holds four consecutive elements along a row, which it can fetch with a single 16-byte vector load, and 16 consecutive threads cover 64 consecutive floats of one row. A warp’s loads are now contiguous: perfectly coalesced.


The program is the same; only the layout attached to the tensor changes. That’s the memory coalescing chapter of my CUDA post, done by a compiler pass.

That’s the pattern for the whole TTGIR pipeline, and reading the list of passes felt like reading the chapter list of my CUDA post. accelerate_matmul changes the layout of a tile-level dot product to one of the special layouts tensor core instructions expect, so it can be lowered to mma or wgmma. remove_layout_conversions minimises the number of times data has to be rearranged between layouts, because each conversion means shuffling values between threads, usually through shared memory. pipeline turns a loop that loads tiles and computes on them into a software pipeline: while the current tile is being computed on, the next few are already being copied asynchronously into shared memory, the multistage buffering from the cp.async chapter, controlled by the num_stages setting. warp_specialize splits warps into producers that move data and consumers that compute, the Hopper pattern. And a set of newer passes handle Blackwell’s tensor memory. Everything I did by hand in that post, Triton does as a pass on a tile-level IR, and that’s only possible because the IR still knows what a tile, a layout and a dot product are.

Part 4: Kernels, and Where They Come From

What Eager PyTorch Actually Does

Before getting to AI compilers, it’s worth being precise about what happens without one. In normal eager PyTorch, every operation runs immediately, as its own kernel. Here’s the journey of a single call like x * a. Python calls into PyTorch’s C++ core. The dispatcher looks at the inputs and works through a stack of dispatch keys: is autograd needed (then record this operation for the backward pass), is there an autocast region active, and finally, which device are the tensors on. It selects the right kernel for that operation, data type and device (a CUDA multiply for float32, say), allocates an output tensor, and launches the kernel. The kernel runs on the GPU and writes a new tensor to memory. Then the next line of Python starts the whole process again.

Each step costs a little: a few microseconds of Python and dispatcher overhead per operation, plus the launch itself. For big tensors that’s negligible next to the kernel. For small ones, or for the decode step of an LLM where hundreds of small kernels run per token, it can dominate, which is exactly why vLLM uses CUDA graphs to replay a whole step with one launch. And there’s a deeper cost: eager mode can never look ahead. When it runs the multiply, it has no idea an add and a ReLU are coming, so it must write the full result to memory for the next kernel to read back.

That’s a deliberate tradeoff, and a good one for research: eager code is easy to write, debug and change, because what you write is exactly what runs, line by line. The cost is everything a compiler could have done with the whole program in view.

Three Sources of Kernels

Every kernel that runs on a GPU comes from one of three places:


Libraries for the heavy maths, hand-written kernels for the patterns that matter most, generated kernels for everything else.

Libraries are where the big matrix multiplies and convolutions come from. cuBLAS isn’t one matmul kernel but hundreds, each tuned for a range of shapes, data types and GPU generations, and a set of heuristics that picks the best one for each call based on the matrix sizes. NVIDIA has spent years on them, and for a large matmul they’re very hard to beat. CUTLASS sits between a library and hand-written code: it’s a collection of C++ templates for the building blocks of a GEMM (tile loaders, tensor core operations, epilogues) that you instantiate for your own case, which is how you get a matmul with your own custom epilogue without writing it from scratch. The limitation of a library is that it’s a fixed menu. cuBLAS can multiply matrices, but it can’t also apply your particular activation function on the way out, unless that combination happens to be on the menu.

Hand-written kernels like FlashAttention are the opposite: the best possible implementation of one specific pattern, written by people who understood something about the problem that no compiler would discover on its own. They’re expensive to write and have to be rewritten as hardware changes, which is why FlashAttention has had four major versions, the later ones each built around the features of a new GPU generation.

Generated kernels come from AI compilers. They cover the long tail: all the elementwise operations, normalisations, reductions and unusual combinations that make up most of a model’s operations by count. A compiler generates a kernel that fits exactly what your model does, fused together, which is often better than any library could offer, and sometimes not quite as good as an expert would write. Real systems use all three at once, and you’ll see that in real compiler output in Part 6.

Part 5: Fusion, the Core Argument

Counting Bytes

If you take one idea from this post, make it this one, because it’s where most of an AI compiler’s speedup comes from. Take y = relu(x · a + b) on a tensor of 100 million 32-bit floats (400 MB), on an H100 with 3.35 TB/s of memory bandwidth, and count the bytes:


Same three FLOPs per element, a third of the memory traffic. On a memory-bound operation, that’s three times the speed.

In eager mode, the multiply reads x (0.4 GB) and writes a temporary tensor (0.4 GB). The add reads that temporary and writes a second one. The ReLU reads the second temporary and writes y. That’s 2.4 GB of traffic, which takes at least 0.72 ms at full bandwidth, plus 0.8 GB of temporary tensors that exist only to be written once, read once and thrown away. A fused kernel reads x once, does all three operations in registers, and writes y once: 0.8 GB, about 0.24 ms, and no temporaries at all.

The roofline from the Tradeoffs post explains why that’s the whole story. Arithmetic intensity is FLOPs per byte moved. Each eager kernel does one floating-point operation per element while moving 8 bytes (4 in, 4 out), an intensity of 0.125 FLOPs per byte. The H100’s ridge point, where compute and bandwidth balance, is around 295 for BF16 tensor core work and far above 0.125 for anything, so each kernel spends almost all its time waiting for memory. The fused kernel does three operations per 8 bytes, an intensity of 0.375: still far below the ridge, still completely memory-bound, so its time is simply its bytes divided by the bandwidth. Fusion doesn’t make the arithmetic any faster. It deletes memory traffic, and for memory-bound work, which describes most of the operations in a model by count, memory traffic is the time.

What Can Be Fused, and What Shouldn’t Be

A chain like this one, where each operation consumes the previous one’s output element by element, is the easy case. Compilers call it pointwise (or elementwise) fusion, and it’s essentially always a win. Three other patterns matter just as much.

Reductions, like the sums and maxima inside a softmax or a layer norm, collapse a dimension, and fusing them with the pointwise work around them is harder, because the reduction has to finish before anything that depends on it can start. A softmax over each row is four operations: the row maximum (a reduction), the exponentials of each element minus that maximum (pointwise), the row sum of those (a reduction), and the division (pointwise). In Part 6 we’ll see Inductor fuse exactly that into one kernel, and do something clever with the intermediate.

Epilogue fusion attaches pointwise work to the output of a matmul. A matmul computes each output tile in registers before writing it to memory; applying the bias, the activation and any scaling to that tile before the write means the unfused output never has to exist in memory at all. This is where generated code and libraries meet, and it’s how a compiler can beat a plain library call.

Horizontal fusion combines independent operations that read the same input, or that are simply small, into one kernel, mainly to save launches and repeated reads.

Fusion isn’t free, though, and a good compiler declines it sometimes. Every operation fused into a kernel needs registers for its intermediate values, and if a kernel needs too many, it either spills to slow memory or runs fewer threads at once (lower occupancy, from the CUDA post), either of which can cost more than the memory traffic saved. If an intermediate result is used by many later operations, fusing it into each of them means recomputing it each time, and past some point recomputing is more expensive than storing it once. And some combinations just don’t fit one kernel’s structure, like two matmuls in a row with incompatible tilings. Deciding what to fuse is really a cost-model problem, and it’s one of the main jobs of Inductor’s scheduler.

FlashAttention, Fusion by Hand

Attention is the famous case where automatic fusion struggles, and it’s worth seeing why. Written naively, attention is a matmul (queries times keys, giving an N × N matrix of scores), a softmax over each row of that matrix, and another matmul (times the values). For a long sequence, that N × N matrix is enormous, and in the unfused version it’s written to memory and read back twice.

The problem is the softmax. To normalise a row, you need its maximum and its sum over all the keys, so it seems you can’t finish any part of a row until you’ve seen the whole thing. FlashAttention, from Tri Dao and colleagues, is built on a reformulation called online softmax (first described by Milakov and Gimelshein in 2018), combined with tiling so that everything happens in on-chip memory. Process the keys in blocks, and keep three running values per row: the maximum m so far, the sum ℓ of exponentials so far, and the partial output O so far. When a new block arrives with a larger maximum m', rescale what you have: multiply ℓ and O by e^(m − m'), which converts every earlier exponential to the new reference point, and then add the new block’s contributions. At the end, divide O by ℓ. The result is mathematically identical to the full softmax, but the N × N matrix never exists: each block of scores is computed, used and discarded inside the kernel.

That rescaling is an algebraic rewrite of softmax that somebody had to discover, and combining it with tiling and careful use of shared memory was the FlashAttention authors’ contribution. It isn’t a scheduling decision a compiler could find by trying different fusions, and that’s why FlashAttention is a hand-written kernel that compilers call, rather than generate, even as they get better at everything around it. It’s also a good reminder of the limits: compilers are very good at applying known transformations exhaustively, and humans are still better at inventing new ones.

Part 6: Inside an AI Compiler

What It Knows That LLVM Doesn’t

An AI compiler isn’t a replacement for LLVM; its output still goes through LLVM, or something like it, to reach the GPU. What makes it different is how much more it knows about the program, because it works on tensors and operations instead of scalars and instructions:


An AI compiler operates one level up, where the graph, the shapes and the meaning of each operation are still visible.

Each row is worth a sentence. Seeing the whole graph is what makes fusion possible: you can only delete an intermediate tensor if you can see everyone who reads it. Knowing what each operation means lets it choose implementations by meaning: a matmul goes to tensor cores, and a softmax can be rewritten into its numerically stable form (subtracting the row maximum first). Knowing shapes at compile time lets it plan every buffer’s memory up front and choose tile sizes that divide the problem evenly. Numeric freedom is a choice the user makes, but a model’s precision tolerances are much looser than C’s, so an AI compiler can reorder reductions or lower precision where a C compiler never could. And the last row, autotuning, is a different philosophy from LLVM’s. LLVM decides between alternatives using a built-in cost model, its best guess at how expensive each instruction is. AI compilers often don’t guess: they compile several versions of a kernel with different tile sizes or warp counts, run each one on the real GPU with the real shapes, and keep the fastest. It’s slow, which is why it’s optional, but it captures effects no cost model predicts.

The most widely used AI compiler today is torch.compile, so let’s follow it end to end, with real output at every stage.

Dynamo: Capturing a Graph From Python

The first problem any PyTorch compiler faces is that a PyTorch program is just Python. There’s no graph to compile, only code that calls tensor operations as it runs, mixed freely with arbitrary Python: loops, conditionals, prints, calls to other libraries. Earlier attempts at compiling PyTorch, like TorchScript, required users to rewrite their code into a restricted subset, and most people didn’t. TorchDynamo, the front end of torch.compile, took a cleverer route: it hooks into the Python interpreter itself.


Dynamo intercepts each Python frame before it runs, pulls the tensor operations out into a graph, and leaves the rest as ordinary Python. Diagram: PyTorch project, BSD-3-Clause

When CPython runs a function, it creates a frame (the function’s local state) and hands it to a frame evaluation function that executes the function’s bytecode, the compact instruction format Python compiles your source into. PEP 523, added in Python 3.6, lets a library replace that evaluation function. Dynamo installs its own. When a compiled function is called, Dynamo reads its bytecode before it runs and symbolically executes it: it steps through each instruction, but instead of real tensors it tracks placeholder values that record what would happen to them. Every tensor operation it encounters is recorded into an FX graph, PyTorch’s simple graph format. Anything that isn’t a tensor operation and can be resolved at compile time (Python arithmetic on constants, attribute lookups, list indexing) is simply evaluated away. Here’s the real FX graph Dynamo captured for torch.relu(x * a + b), called with a tensor of 1,024 floats, a = 2.0 and b = 1.0:

Three operations, each annotated with its exact type, shape and stride (f32[1024][1] means float32, shape 1024, stride 1). The = None assignments tell later stages exactly when each intermediate stops being needed. And notice that a and b are gone: Dynamo saw they were plain Python floats and specialised on them, baking 2.0 and 1.0 straight into the graph as constants.

Guards: The Price of Assumptions

Specialising on a = 2.0 and a size of 1,024 makes the compiled code simpler and faster, but it’s only correct for calls that match those assumptions. So alongside every compiled graph, Dynamo installs guards: checks that run before each call to decide whether the cached code still applies. Here are the real guards for that first compilation, trimmed:

Every assumption the compiled code depends on is checked. a and b must still equal the values that were baked in. x must still be a float32 CPU tensor of exactly this size and stride, not requiring gradients. Global settings, like whether gradients are enabled, must be unchanged. There’s even an ID_MATCH on torch.relu itself, because Python would let you replace torch.relu with some other function, and the compiled code assumed the real one. The last line is a reminder that this is real overhead: here, about 100 microseconds per call, which is negligible for a large model and significant for a tiny one.

When a guard fails, Dynamo recompiles. I called the same compiled function with a tensor of 2,048 elements, then 4,096, then with a = 3.0. Here’s what Dynamo logged:

The second size didn’t just trigger a recompile with a new fixed size. Dynamo noticed that the size had changed and assumed it would keep changing, a heuristic called automatic dynamic shapes. The recompiled graph uses a symbolic size instead of a number:

s77 is a symbolic integer, a stand-in for “whatever the size turns out to be”, and the guard on x became much looser: size=[None], plus a single condition, 2 <= L['x'].size()[0]. (Sizes 0 and 1 are excluded because PyTorch’s broadcasting rules treat a dimension of size 1 specially, so they’d need different code.) That’s why the call with 4,096 elements didn’t recompile at all: it satisfied the dynamic guards. The generated kernel changed too, taking the size as a parameter ks0 and adding a tail loop that handles the last few elements when the size isn’t a multiple of the vector width of 8. Dynamic code is a little slower than code specialised for one exact size, which is the tradeoff behind the whole mechanism. The call with a = 3.0 failed the remaining guard and compiled a third version.

When Dynamo hits something it can’t trace, like a print(), it splits the function there. That’s a graph break. I tried a function that multiplies a tensor, prints something, and then takes a sine. Dynamo’s own report: one graph break, two graphs, reason “Failed to trace builtin operator”. The print runs as ordinary Python between two compiled graphs. Graph breaks are correct and silent, but every one ends a graph, and fusion can’t cross the break, so finding and removing them is most of the practical work of making a model compile well.

AOTAutograd: Planning the Backward Pass

The FX graph then goes through the rest of the stack:


Dynamo captures, AOTAutograd prepares forward and backward graphs ahead of time, and Inductor generates the kernels. Diagram: PyTorch project, BSD-3-Clause

AOTAutograd (“ahead-of-time autograd”) handles training. In eager mode, PyTorch builds the backward pass dynamically: as each forward operation runs, it records what’s needed to compute its gradient later. AOTAutograd instead traces the backward computation ahead of time, producing a forward graph and a backward graph that the compiler can optimise together. On the way, it does three important things. It decomposes PyTorch’s thousands of operators into a much smaller core set, so the back end has far fewer operations to understand. It functionalises the graph, rewriting in-place mutations (x.add_(1)) as pure operations that return new values, which makes the graph much easier to analyse and fuse. And it partitions the work between forward and backward, deciding which intermediate results the forward pass should save for the backward pass to use.

I compiled a small training step, gelu(x @ w).sum() with gradients needed for w, to watch this. Here’s the real forward graph:

gelu no longer exists as a single operation: it has been decomposed into its definition, 0.5 · x · (1 + erf(x / √2)), using only core operations that the back end knows how to fuse. And look at what the forward graph returns besides the loss: mm and permute. Those are the saved tensors, the only things the backward pass will receive. Now the backward graph:

Two things are worth noticing. First, this is real calculus: the derivative of gelu is ½(1 + erf(x/√2)) + x · φ(x), where φ is the standard normal density, and 0.3989… is 1/√(2π), the constant in front of it. Second, and more interesting: the backward graph recomputes erf from mm, instead of receiving it from the forward pass. The forward pass computed erf too, but the partitioner decided it was cheaper to store only mm and recompute the cheap pointwise operations from it than to write the extra intermediate tensors to memory and read them back. This is rematerialisation, and PyTorch’s partitioner chooses which tensors to save by solving a min-cut problem over the graph, with memory traffic as the cost. It’s the same “recompute or store” tradeoff as fusion, made automatically, across the boundary between forward and backward. (The None is the gradient for x, which didn’t need one.)

Inductor: Fusion, Memory and Code

TorchInductor is the back end that turns the graph into code. It lowers each operation into a loop-level IR: a description of each output as “for every index, compute this expression from these inputs”, which makes fusion a matter of checking whether two loop nests can be merged. Its scheduler then decides which nodes to fuse into which kernels, plans memory so buffers are reused, and finally generates code: Triton kernels on GPUs, and C++ with OpenMP and vector intrinsics on CPUs.

What It Costs

None of this is free, and the costs explain a lot about how systems like vLLM are built. The first call of my compiled function took 68 seconds on a cold cache, most of it spent generating C++ and compiling it with the system’s C++ compiler. The second call took about 1.3 milliseconds. Real models take minutes to compile, which is why vLLM caches its compiled artifacts on disk, and why serving systems care so much about cold starts. Every call pays for its guards, around 100 µs in my example. Every new input shape can trigger a recompile, until automatic dynamic shapes kick in, and dynamic code is a little slower than specialised code. Graph breaks quietly cost performance, because fusion stops at each one. And debugging gets harder, because the code that runs is no longer the code you wrote. None of these are reasons not to compile, but they’re why torch.compile is opt-in, and why it has so many knobs.

The Rest of the Landscape

torch.compile isn’t the only AI compiler, and the others make different choices along the same axes.

XLA, from Google, compiles whole programs for JAX and TensorFlow through its own graph IR, HLO, now standardised as StableHLO under the OpenXLA project. JAX programs are traced as pure functions, with no arbitrary Python side effects inside them, so XLA always sees the whole function at once and never needs graph breaks. Compiled programs are cached by the shapes and types of their inputs, which plays the same role as Dynamo’s guards. It fuses aggressively, and it’s the compiler for Google’s TPUs.

TensorRT, from NVIDIA, works ahead of time on a trained model, for deployment rather than development. Its builder fuses layers, chooses precisions (including INT8 versions calibrated against sample data, and FP8), and benchmarks many candidate kernel implementations, which it calls tactics, for every layer on the exact GPU you’ll deploy to. Building an engine can take a long time, and the result is specific to one GPU model, but it’s fast.

TVM, from academia and now an Apache project, was one of the first to generate tuned kernels automatically for many kinds of hardware, from server GPUs to phones. It separates what to compute from how to schedule it, and searches over schedules with machine-learned cost models.

IREE is built on MLIR from end to end, and targets everything from data-centre GPUs to mobile devices, with its own runtime.

And Triton sits underneath several of these as the kernel language: the level at which generated code is written, whether a human or Inductor writes it.

Part 7: The Bottom of the Stack

Teaching a Compiler a New Chip

At the very bottom of the map, someone has to teach the compiler about each piece of hardware. In LLVM, that’s a back end, and most of a back end isn’t written as C++ but described in TableGen, a small language for writing down a target’s instructions, registers and calling conventions. At build time, a tool called tblgen turns those descriptions into the C++ tables the rest of LLVM uses:


Most of a back end is a description of the hardware, written once in TableGen and turned into code automatically. Figure: Chris Lattner, The Architecture of Open Source Applications (2011), CC BY 3.0

To see what that looks like, I read the NVPTX back end’s instruction descriptions in the LLVM repository. Here’s a real excerpt that defines the floating-point add, subtract and multiply instructions:

A multiclass is a template that stamps out a family of instruction definitions (F3 produces versions for f64, f32, f16, bf16 and their two-wide vector forms), each paired with a pattern that says which IR operations it implements. The pattern fragment BinOpAllowsFMA contains a tiny piece of C++ that runs during instruction selection, and look at what it checks: hasAllowContract(). That’s the contract flag on the fmul and fadd in our kernel’s IR from Part 2. If the flag is set, LLVM is allowed to fuse the pair into an FMA itself, which is exactly what happened in our kernel’s PTX, and any add or multiply left over is emitted in its plain form, leaving ptxas free to fuse it later. If the flag isn’t set, the back end uses the .rn versions instead, which have an explicit rounding mode and, as a comment in the file explains, stop ptxas from fusing them. A permission granted by the CUDA front end, recorded on an IR instruction, finally becomes a choice between two PTX instructions in a pattern here.

A few hundred lines further on, there’s a definition I didn’t expect: an instruction called fma.rn.relu, available on sm_80 and newer for FP16 and BF16, which computes max(a × b + c, 0) in one instruction. That’s our entire kernel. Its pattern only matches if the FMA has a single use and the operations carry two fast-math flags: nnan (no NaNs) and nsz (no signed zeros). To check, I compiled the computation in BF16 for sm_80 twice, once with those flags and once without:

One instruction instead of two, but only with permission, because the fused instruction treats NaN inputs and negative zero differently from a separate FMA and max. That’s the as-if rule from the very start of this post, at the very bottom of the stack: the back end will only use the faster instruction when it can prove, or has been promised, that the difference can’t be observed.

That’s what teaching LLVM a new chip mostly is. Instruction definitions with their patterns, so the instruction selector can match IR to them. Register classes, so the register allocator knows what it has to work with. A scheduling model, describing each instruction’s latency and which execution units it uses. Encodings, so the MC layer can emit bytes. And for genuinely new hardware, like a tensor core with a new data type, new intrinsics, so front ends can reach the new instructions directly.

The Memory Model, Where Correctness Lives

The part of a back end I found most interesting is the least visible one: the memory model. Start with a simple pattern called message passing. One thread writes some data and then sets a flag; another thread waits until it sees the flag set, and then reads the data. In source code, it looks obviously correct. On real hardware, it isn’t, unless the compiler emits the right instructions.

The reason is that modern processors don’t make writes visible to everyone in the order they happen. A write might sit in a store buffer, or in a cache that other cores can’t see, and two writes from the same thread can become visible to another thread in the opposite order. GPUs are especially relaxed about this, because enforcing a single global order would cost enormous bandwidth. So languages provide atomic operations with ordering. A release store says: everything I wrote before this store must be visible before the store itself is. An acquire load says: nothing I read after this load may see older values than the ones this load saw. Put a release on the flag write and an acquire on the flag read, and the message-passing pattern is guaranteed to work.


Without ordering, the flag can arrive before the data. Release and acquire turn that into a guarantee, and the back end decides what it costs.

There’s one more ingredient: scope, meaning “visible to whom?”. On a GPU, the threads that must see a write might be in the same workgroup, sharing one compute unit’s L1 cache, or anywhere on the device, or on other devices and the CPU. A wider scope means more of the cache hierarchy has to be made consistent, and that costs more. AMD’s MI300X makes this vivid, because it’s built from several compute chiplets, each with its own L2 cache. A write that only needs to be seen within a workgroup stays in one compute unit and needs no cache maintenance at all. A write that must be seen by the whole device has to be pushed out of one chiplet’s L2 so the others can see it.

To see what the back end actually does, I compiled the same few lines of LLVM IR, a store of the data followed by an atomic release store of the flag at different scopes, for MI300 (gfx942) and for NVIDIA (sm_89):


The wider the scope, the more caches have to be written back or invalidated. The back end emits exactly as much as that scope needs.

Reading the AMD code with the release and acquire definitions in hand, every line has a reason. buffer_wbl2 (“write back L2”) pushes this chiplet’s dirty L2 cache lines out to memory, so a reader on another chiplet can see them; that’s the “everything before is visible first” half of release. s_waitcnt vmcnt(0) stalls until all of this thread’s outstanding vector memory operations have completed, so the flag store can’t overtake the data. On the reader’s side, buffer_inv invalidates the reader’s L2 lines for other chiplets’ memory, so the following data load can’t be served a stale copy; that’s acquire. The sc0 and sc1 bits on the instructions select the cache coherence behaviour, and the wider the scope, the more of them are set. At workgroup scope none of this is needed, and the back end emits none of it. LLVM’s documentation for the AMD back end specifies these sequences chip by chip, over thousands of lines, because they depend on exactly how each chip’s caches are built.

NVIDIA’s PTX takes the other approach: the scope is part of the instruction (st.release.gpu, ld.acquire.gpu), and the cache work happens below PTX, in ptxas and the hardware. Both designs are correct. What isn’t negotiable is that the back end gets it exactly right for every chip and every scope, because a mistake here doesn’t crash. It produces a program that passes every test on one GPU, and silently reads stale data on another.

Why Compilers Are Having a Moment

Step back, and the M × N problem from Part 2 has returned, one level up. There are a handful of ML frameworks and a fast-growing list of accelerators: NVIDIA and AMD GPUs, Google’s TPUs, AWS’s Trainium, Groq, Cerebras, and in-house chips at almost every large AI company. Each of those chips is useless without a compiler stack that takes models from the frameworks people actually use and turns them into fast code for that hardware, with the right fusions, the right layouts, the right instructions and a correct memory model. The hardware is the visible part. The compiler often decides whether anyone can use it.

Putting It Together

Follow a PyTorch model all the way down the map. Dynamo intercepts the Python bytecode, symbolically executes it, records the tensor operations into an FX graph, specialises on what it can and guards every assumption. AOTAutograd traces the backward pass ahead of time, decomposes thousands of operators into a small core set, and decides what to save for the backward pass and what to recompute. Inductor lowers the graph to loops, fuses everything its cost model allows, replaces intermediates with small scratch buffers or removes them entirely, reuses memory, hands the heavy matmuls to cuBLAS, and generates the rest as Triton kernels. The Triton compiler assigns every tile a layout, coalesces the loads, maps dot products onto tensor cores and pipelines the copies, then lowers to LLVM IR. LLVM’s NVPTX back end selects instructions by pattern, fusing multiply-adds where the contract flag allows, and emits PTX. And ptxas allocates registers and schedules instructions for one specific GPU, producing the SASS that runs. Underneath it all, each back end knows exactly which caches to flush for every atomic, at every scope.

At every level, the theme is the same: a compiler can only optimise what it can still see, and it can only do what the meaning of the program permits. LLVM can vectorise a loop but not fuse two kernels, because it never sees the graph. MLIR exists so that a matmul stays a matmul long enough to be tiled. AI compilers exist because the biggest wins, fusion above all, need the whole graph, the shapes and the meaning of every operation. And at the bottom, a single fast instruction like fma.rn.relu is only used when the program has given permission for the difference to be invisible.

Where the Map Leaves Me

What I like most about this stack is that it’s the same set of tradeoffs as the hardware underneath it, one level of abstraction up. Fusion is the memory wall again: an AI compiler’s main job is to stop moving bytes it doesn’t need to, which is exactly what my CUDA post spent twenty-five chapters doing by hand. Triton’s passes are that post’s chapters, automated. Rematerialisation is the “save or recompute” decision every cache designer makes. Even the memory model at the very bottom is the cache hierarchy from Ahead of the Pipeline, turned into rules a compiler has to follow. Compilers turn out to be where hardware knowledge gets encoded so that nobody has to apply it by hand again.

It also changed how I think about the work itself. The common picture is that people who understand hardware write kernels, and people who understand ML write models. The compiler is where those two meet, and it needs people who understand both: enough about tensors and graphs to know what can be fused, and enough about caches, instruction sets and memory ordering to know what the result should look like. Every new accelerator needs a team like that, which is probably why compiler engineering for ML has become one of the more interesting places to work right now.

And it was satisfying in a very basic way. Generating real IR, guards and SASS on my own laptop, and seeing the four accumulators appear in vectorised code, the erf get recomputed in a backward graph, a whole softmax intermediate shrink to a 4 KB buffer, or a buffer_wbl2 appear at exactly the scope that needs it, made the whole map feel concrete in a way reading about it never did. If you want to try it, Compiler Explorer and a five-line function are all it takes, and TORCH_LOGS will show you everything else.
